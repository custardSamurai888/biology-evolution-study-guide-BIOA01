BIO A01 | Evolution Study Guide

Evolution, species & the history of life

A plain-language guide to the sections on your schedule, followed by a quiz that explains every answer as soon as you choose.

Big picture: populations change across generations when inherited variants become more or less common. Over long periods, those changes produce adaptations, new species, and the branching diversity of life. Fossils, genes, body structures, and development let us reconstruct that history.

1. Evolution starts with heritable variation

Chapter 1.4

What evolution means

Evolution is a change in inherited traits in a population over generations. Individuals do not evolve during their own lives; populations do. Natural selection can make a population better suited to its current environment, but evolution has no plan or guaranteed direction.

Individuals differ. Some differences are inherited because of DNA variants (alleles); others come from environment and are not passed on. Mutation creates new alleles, while recombination reshuffles existing ones. Genetic variation is the raw material evolution can act on.

Two ways to describe variation: a gene is polymorphic when multiple alleles occur in a population; nucleotide diversity measures DNA sequence differences among sampled copies. Variation within a population matters because it gives populations options when conditions change.

2. Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

Chapter 20.3

Allele frequencies and genotype frequencies

For a gene with two alleles, call their frequencies p and q; together they equal 1. If alleles combine randomly, expected genotype frequencies are p² (AA), 2pq (Aa), and q² (aa), adding to 1. Example: if q = 0.2, then p = 0.8 and expected aa frequency is q² = 0.04.

This Hardy–Weinberg pattern is a baseline, not a claim that real populations never change. It assumes no differences in survival or reproduction among genotypes (no selection), a very large population (little drift), no migration, no mutation, and random mating. When these conditions hold, allele and genotype frequencies stay stable from generation to generation. A departure tells us at least one assumption may fail; it does not, by itself, identify the cause. Useful nuance: nonrandom mating can change genotype frequencies without immediately changing allele frequencies. For example, mating among relatives can increase homozygotes and decrease heterozygotes. When these conditions hold, allele frequencies stay stable from generation to generation. A departure can signal evolution or a violated assumption; investigate which cause fits.

3. The forces that change populations

Chapters 20.4

Selection, drift, gene flow, mutation

Natural selection

Heritable traits that help carriers leave more surviving offspring tend to become more common. Selection acts on phenotypes, but evolution is measured as changes in inherited variants. Fitness means reproductive success in a particular environment.

Genetic drift

Allele frequencies shift by chance, especially in small populations. A bottleneck sharply reduces population size; a founder event occurs when a few individuals start a new population. Either can lose variation without regard to usefulness.

Gene flow

Alleles move between populations when individuals or gametes migrate and reproduce. It often makes populations more genetically similar and can add variation to a population.

Mutation

Mutation is the original source of new alleles. It usually changes frequencies slowly by itself, but it supplies new variants for selection and drift to act on.

Molecular evolution tracks DNA or protein sequence changes. Some mutations alter function and are selected; many are neutral or nearly neutral and can spread by drift. — Neutral — means no meaningful effect on reproductive success in the studied setting, not that the sequence is unimportant in every context.

Natural selection: the core idea

Natural selection happens when individuals vary, some of that variation is inherited, and individuals with certain inherited traits leave more offspring. Over generations, alleles linked to greater reproductive success become more common. Individuals do not evolve; populations do. Selection has no plan and can only favor traits that help in the current environment.

Fitness means reproductive contribution compared with others in the same environment, not strength or health in general. A trait can be helpful in one environment and harmful in another. Positive selection raises a beneficial allele; negative selection removes harmful effects. A harmful recessive allele can remain hidden in healthy heterozygotes.

Patterns that shape variation

Directional selection favors one end of a trait range (for example, larger beaks when drought leaves mostly hard seeds). Stabilizing selection favors intermediate values and acts against extremes (such as intermediate birth weight). Disruptive selection favors both ends over the middle. Balancing selection maintains multiple alleles, for example when heterozygotes have an advantage: sickle-cell trait can protect against malaria without causing severe sickle-cell disease.

Artificial selection occurs when people choose which organisms reproduce. Sexual selection is selection related to getting mates: competition among members of one sex is intrasexual selection; mate choice is intersexual selection. Both may favor traits that help reproduction even if they carry survival costs.

3A. Other ways populations evolve

Chapter 20.5

Genetic drift is a chance-driven change in allele frequencies. It has a stronger effect in small populations and can randomly remove variation. A bottleneck happens when a population suddenly shrinks; a founder event happens when a few individuals start a new population. In either case, the small sample may have very different allele frequencies from the original population.

Migration moves individuals between populations; gene flow is the movement of their alleles. Gene flow can make populations more genetically alike. Mutation is the source of new alleles and the raw material for evolution, though it usually changes frequencies slowly. Nonrandom mating, including inbreeding, changes genotype proportions: it increases homozygotes and decreases heterozygotes, but by itself does not change allele frequencies. Inbreeding can expose harmful recessive alleles and reduce fitness (inbreeding depression).

These forces can change populations, but drift, mutation, migration, and nonrandom mating do not by themselves create adaptations. Natural selection is the mechanism that can produce adaptation because it consistently favors inherited traits that improve reproductive success in a particular environment.

3B. Molecular evolution and molecular clocks

Chapter 20.6

Molecular evolution is the accumulation of genetic differences in lineages over time. Once populations become isolated, mutations arise separately in each lineage; different changes may be lost, selected for, or become fixed. The longer two lineages have been separate, the more chances there have been for differences to accumulate.

A molecular clock uses sequence differences between species to estimate when they diverged. Scientists calibrate the clock with dates from fossils or other known events. The clock is an estimate, not a perfect stopwatch: mutation and evolutionary rates vary among genes. Strong negative selection removes many harmful changes and makes a gene evolve slowly (histone genes are an example). A nonfunctional pseudogene has little or no functional constraint, so neutral mutations can accumulate quickly.

Species: what counts as a species?

Chapter 21.1

The biological species concept (BSC) defines a species as populations that actually or potentially interbreed and produce viable, fertile offspring, while being reproductively isolated from other groups. A horse and donkey can produce a mule, but mules are infertile, so the parents belong to different species. Populations can still be the same species even if they live apart, if they could interbreed if brought together.

The BSC is useful for understanding gene flow and speciation, but difficult to test in practice. It does not work well for asexual organisms or fossils, and hybridization can blur boundaries. The morphospecies concept groups organisms by shared physical traits; it can miss cryptic species that look alike and can split one species whose members look different (such as males and females). The ecological species concept emphasizes a distinct ecological niche; the phylogenetic species concept emphasizes shared ancestry and a distinct lineage. No single definition fits every case perfectly.

Reproductive isolation: barriers to gene flow

Chapter 21.2

Prezygotic barriers act before fertilization, preventing mating or the joining of egg and sperm. They include geographic separation (different places), ecological separation (different habitats or host plants), temporal separation (different breeding times), behavioral differences (courtship signals do not match), mechanical incompatibility (bodies cannot mate), and gametic incompatibility (sperm and egg cannot fuse).

Postzygotic barriers act after fertilization. A hybrid embryo may fail to develop (hybrid inviability), or the hybrid may survive but be infertile (hybrid sterility), like a mule. Both kinds of barriers reduce gene flow between populations. Prezygotic barriers prevent the wasted effort of producing a hybrid that will not reproduce, but either kind can contribute to reproductive isolation and the formation of separate species.

How new species form

Chapter 21.3

Speciation is the evolution of reproductive isolation. When populations stop exchanging genes, they accumulate different mutations; selection and genetic drift can make those differences grow. Eventually, they may no longer produce viable, fertile offspring. This can happen gradually, and natural selection is not always required.

Allopatric speciation begins with geographic separation. Dispersal is when some individuals move to a new place; vicariance is when a new barrier splits a population already living in an area. A small isolated population at the edge of the range can undergo peripatric speciation, where strong drift and different local conditions may speed divergence. Sympatric speciation occurs without geographic separation; disruptive selection can help if hybrids have low fitness and selection counteracts gene flow.

Adaptive radiation is rapid diversification when a lineage encounters many underused ecological opportunities, as with Darwin — s finches. Co-speciation occurs when interacting lineages (such as hosts and parasites) split in response to each other. In plants, hybridization followed by chromosome doubling (polyploidy) can create reproductive isolation in one generation. Speciation can involve selection, drift, or both.

Reading evolutionary trees

Chapter 22.1

A phylogeny is the branching history of a group. A phylogenetic tree is a testable hypothesis about relatedness, based on evidence such as anatomy, fossils, and DNA. Time generally runs from the root toward the branch tips. A branch point, or node, represents a common ancestor. Two groups are more closely related if they share a more recent common ancestor. Those closest relatives are sister groups.

Do not judge relatedness by how close names appear on the page or by whether one tip is higher or lower. Branches can rotate around a node without changing relationships. Living species at the tips have all been evolving for the same amount of time since their ancestors split; a modern species is not — more evolved — than another.

A monophyletic group (clade) contains one ancestor and all its descendants. A paraphyletic group leaves out some descendants; — fish — excluding tetrapods is a common example. A polyphyletic group combines organisms without including their most recent shared ancestor, often based on similar traits that evolved independently. Modern taxonomy aims to name clades and organizes groups in a nested hierarchy.

How biologists build evolutionary trees

Chapter 22.2

Biologists build trees by comparing characters (features such as body structures, behaviors, or DNA) and their character states (the forms those features take). Homology is similarity inherited from a common ancestor and is evidence of shared ancestry. Analogy is similarity that evolved independently, often because of similar environments (convergent evolution), so it can mislead tree building.

The most useful clues are synapomorphies: shared derived traits found in some, but not all, members of the group being studied. They point to a common ancestor that acquired the trait. Biologists compare an ingroup (the groups whose relationships they want to resolve) with an outgroup (a related group outside it) to help identify which traits are ancestral versus newly evolved. Cladistics groups organisms using these shared derived traits.

When several trees could fit the evidence, parsimony favors the tree that requires the fewest evolutionary changes. Trees are hypotheses, so new evidence can change them. DNA and protein sequences offer many characters and are especially useful when visible traits are limited; molecular and anatomical evidence can be combined. Avoid assuming that every similar trait signals close relatedness.

What fossils tell us

Chapter 22.3

Fossils are preserved remains or traces of past organisms. They provide direct evidence of ancient life, show extinct organisms that living-species trees cannot include, and can reveal transitional combinations of traits. For example, fossils such as Tiktaalik show a mix of fish and land-vertebrate features, while fossils linking birds and dinosaurs show how traits accumulated along branches.

The fossil record is incomplete and biased: an organism is more likely to fossilize if it is rapidly buried in a sediment-forming environment and has hard parts that resist decay. Marine organisms with shells are often preserved more readily than soft-bodied land organisms. Trace fossils record activity such as footprints or burrows; molecular fossils are preserved biological molecules or chemical traces.

Rock layers can show relative order: lower layers are generally older than layers above them. Radiometric dating measures predictable radioactive decay in minerals to estimate an absolute age, helping calibrate the sequence of fossils and evolutionary events. Fossils also preserve clues about past environments and show how environmental changes and extinctions have reshaped life. Mass extinctions remove many lineages; survivors may then diversify into newly available ecological opportunities.

The big patterns in life's history

Chapter 22.4

Scientists reconstruct life's history by combining two kinds of evidence: phylogenies, built from living organisms and their traits or DNA, and the fossil record, which shows extinct organisms and the environments they lived in. These independent sources often support the same branching story.

Life's deep history is largely microbial. Bacteria and archaea were diverse long before complex eukaryotic cells and animals appeared. Photosynthetic microbes changed Earth's environment by releasing oxygen; rising oxygen made new ways of life possible and helped support the evolution of eukaryotic cells and more complex organisms.

Life later diversified in the oceans and colonized land in multiple lineages. Plants changed land environments and created food and habitats that helped other organisms thrive there. Evolution is not a straight march toward better life: lineages branch, adapt to changing environments, and sometimes disappear. Mass extinctions eliminate many groups, but survivors can diversify into newly open ecological roles. The shared story from fossils, living organisms, and environmental evidence is powerful evidence for evolution.

Humans and the great apes

Chapter 23.1

Humans are primates, a mammal group with traits such as forward-facing eyes that help with depth perception, grasping hands with flexible thumbs, nails rather than claws, and relatively large brains. Within primates, apes include gibbons and the great apes: orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees and bonobos, and humans. Humans belong inside the great-ape branch.

DNA comparisons show that humans' closest living relatives are chimpanzees and bonobos. Humans did not descend from modern chimpanzees; both lineages descended from a shared ancestor. Molecular evidence helps resolve relationships that anatomy alone may not distinguish. Fossils add direct evidence about extinct hominins (members of the human-side lineage) and reveal combinations of traits, such as upright walking alongside a smaller brain.

Human evolution is a branching tree, not a ladder: multiple hominin lineages existed, and many ended without leading to humans today. Fossils can be incomplete and scientists may disagree about how to classify them, but the broad picture comes from combining fossil, anatomical, and molecular evidence.

Why eukaryotic cells are different

Chapter 25.1

Eukaryotic cells have a flexible internal cytoskeleton (protein scaffolding) and a connected endomembrane system that includes the nuclear envelope, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi, and cell membrane. Together these structures organize the cell, move materials, and let cells change shape. Eukaryotes can take in particles or other cells by endocytosis (including phagocytosis) and release materials by exocytosis. Internal transport also helps eukaryotic cells grow larger than most prokaryotic cells.

Energy metabolism is compartmentalized: mitochondria carry out most aerobic respiration, and chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis. Eukaryotes can use a wider range of food sources, including engulfing other cells, and flexible cell structures make specialized shapes and cell types possible. Their multiple linear chromosomes can be copied from many starting points, and regulatory DNA helps control gene expression and complex life cycles.

Most eukaryotes can reproduce sexually at least sometimes. Meiosis makes genetically varied haploid cells; fertilization joins two haploid cells to form a diploid zygote. The balance between haploid and diploid stages differs across eukaryotes: animals are mostly diploid with haploid gametes, while plants have multicellular haploid and diploid stages.

Where eukaryotic cells came from

Chapter 25.2 â€¢ Endosymbiosis

Endosymbiosis means one organism lives inside another. The leading explanation for mitochondria and chloroplasts is that ancestral bacteria were engulfed by another cell and gradually became permanent partners. Mitochondria descended from bacteria related to proteobacteria; chloroplasts descended from photosynthetic cyanobacteria.

Several clues support this idea: mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own small, circular DNA; their genes and protein-making machinery resemble those of bacteria; they are surrounded by two membranes; and they reproduce by dividing inside the cell. Over evolutionary time, many genes from these bacterial partners moved to the host cell's nucleus. Even eukaryotes that lack typical mitochondria have genetic evidence that their ancestors once had them.

Evidence also suggests that the host lineage was archaeal or closely related to archaea. Scientists still debate whether a complex host engulfed a bacterium or whether the partnership itself gave rise to the eukaryotic cell. The exact path is not fully settled, but the hybrid nature of eukaryotic cells and their organelles strongly supports a symbiotic origin.

Eukaryotic diversity: many different solutions

Chapter 25.3

How to make sense of eukaryote diversity

Eukaryotes include animals, fungi, plants, and many mostly microscopic groups. “Protist” is a handy informal label for diverse eukaryotes, but it does not name one complete branch of the evolutionary tree. Similar lifestyles or shapes can evolve separately, so scientists use DNA and other evidence to infer relationships.

Opisthokonts include animals, fungi, and relatives such as choanoflagellates. Choanoflagellates are especially close living relatives of animals, and their genes offer clues to the older toolkit from which animal biology evolved. Amoebozoans move or feed with extensions called pseudopodia; some slime molds can temporarily form coordinated multicellular structures.

Archaeplastids include red algae, green algae, and land plants. Their ancestor acquired a cyanobacterium that became a chloroplast. Green algae and land plants share important cellular and genetic traits. Other major eukaryote groups include SAR: stramenopiles, alveolates, and rhizarians. Examples include kelp and diatoms (stramenopiles), ciliates and dinoflagellates (alveolates), and many diverse microscopic forms.

Photosynthesis occurs on several separate branches of the eukaryotic tree because photosynthetic cells were taken up by other eukaryotes in later endosymbioses. When one eukaryote engulfs a photosynthetic eukaryote, the chloroplast may be retained; this is secondary endosymbiosis. This repeated transfer explains why chloroplast and nuclear DNA can tell different relationship stories.

Eukaryotic evolutionary history

Chapter 25.4

What fossils and living ecosystems reveal

The eukaryotic fossil record is incomplete. Many single-celled organisms leave no durable remains, so fossils that can be confidently recognized as eukaryotic often preserve distinctive cell walls or detailed shapes. Complex wall patterns and internal layering point to cell machinery such as a cytoskeleton and endomembrane system.

Fossils show that eukaryotes diversified over time. Algae and simple multicellular forms appeared, and later many new kinds of microscopic eukaryotes became common in oceans. A possible driver was an ecological arms race: once some protists began eating other protists, prey and predators could evolve new ways to capture food or avoid being eaten. Changing nutrient availability may also have helped photosynthetic algae expand.

Some marine protists make mineral shells: foraminiferans use calcium carbonate, while radiolarians use silica. Diatoms and other microscopic algae contribute substantially to ocean photosynthesis and help move carbon and silica into seafloor sediments. Overall, tiny eukaryotes have shaped food webs, ocean chemistry, and habitats for other organisms.

Development begins with one cell

Chapter 18.1

How cells with the same DNA become different

A fertilized egg is totipotent: it can produce all the cell types needed for a complete organism, including supporting tissues. As cells divide and develop, they become more specialized and their possible fates narrow. Pluripotent cells can make any body cell type, while multipotent cells can make a more limited set of related cell types.

Most cells in an organism keep essentially the same genome. They become different because different genes are switched on or off. Experiments transferring a nucleus from a specialized cell into an egg show that the nucleus still contains the instructions for building an organism; the egg’s cytoplasm can sometimes reset gene activity. Thus, differentiation usually reflects gene regulation rather than the loss of genes.

Scientists can also reprogram some adult cells into induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells by activating a small set of regulatory genes. These cells can make many body cell types and may support future therapies. Reprogramming must be carefully controlled because incomplete changes or abnormal growth can create risks.

Developmental genes work in a hierarchy

Chapter 18.2

Early instructions guide later details

Developmental control is hierarchical: genes active early control later groups of genes. In fruit flies, maternal-effect genes put information into the egg before fertilization. For example, Bicoid is concentrated near the front and Nanos near the rear. Their protein gradients help establish the embryo’s head-to-tail direction and regulate other genes.

Then the embryo’s own genes refine the pattern in steps. Gap genes mark broad regions; pair-rule genes divide the embryo into alternating stripes; segment-polarity genes refine each segment and its front/back pattern. Each level helps control the next. If an early level is disrupted, many later structures may be affected.

Hox (homeotic) genes specify the identity of body regions: they help determine which structures form in each segment. A Hox gene expressed in the wrong place can cause a body part to develop in the wrong location. Similar Hox genes are found across many animals, showing that evolution has reused an ancient developmental toolkit. In many Hox clusters, gene order along a chromosome corresponds broadly to the body regions and sequence of expression they influence.

4. Evidence for evolution

Chapter 22 introduction & 22.3

Many independent clues tell the same story

Fossils document organisms through time. Their order in rock layers shows that life has changed; radiometric dating estimates ages from predictable radioactive decay. The fossil record is incomplete, but it includes transitional forms and major patterns of appearance and extinction.

Comparative anatomy reveals homology: similarities inherited from a common ancestor, even when structures now do different jobs (such as vertebrate forelimbs). Analogous features have similar functions that evolved independently. Vestigial features are reduced remnants of ancestral structures.

Biogeography — where organisms live — fits descent with modification: isolation, continental history, and local environments shape related lineages. DNA and protein comparisons often provide especially detailed evidence of relatedness. Similarities across fossils, anatomy, geography, and molecules make a coherent case for common ancestry.

Humans are primates and great apes. Humans did not descend from modern chimpanzees; humans and other living apes share ancestors. Evolutionary trees place humans within the broader primate branch.

5. Classification and phylogeny

Chapter 22.1

How to read the family tree of life

A phylogenetic tree is a hypothesis about relationships. Branch points (nodes) represent common ancestors. Two lineages are close relatives when they share a more recent common ancestor — not because their tips are drawn close together. Rotating branches around a node does not change the relationships. Unless the tree has a time scale, branch length may not represent time or amount of change.

A clade is one ancestor and all its descendants. Shared derived traits help identify clades. Scientists compare many traits — especially DNA sequences — and use an outgroup (a related lineage outside the group) to help infer which traits are ancestral. Classification aims to name evolutionary groups, not just organisms that look alike.

6. What counts as a species? How do new ones form?

Chapter 21.1

Species boundaries and reproductive isolation

The biological species concept defines species as populations that can interbreed in nature and are reproductively isolated from other such groups. It is useful for living, sexually reproducing organisms, but harder to apply to fossils, asexual organisms, or geographically separated populations. Other concepts use shared ancestry, ecological role, or genetic clustering.

Prezygotic barriers prevent mating or fertilization (different habitats, timing, courtship, mechanical fit, or incompatible gametes). Postzygotic barriers act after fertilization (hybrid fails to develop, is weak, or is sterile). These barriers reduce gene flow.

Allopatric speciation: a geographic barrier separates populations; mutation, selection, and drift make them diverge until reproductive isolation evolves. Sympatric speciation: new species form without a geographic split, for example through polyploidy in plants or strong ecological/mating differences. In either case, reduced gene flow lets lineages diverge.

7. Eukaryotic cells: origins and diversity

Chapter 25

A cell assembled through deep evolutionary history

Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and internal membrane-bound compartments. The leading explanation for mitochondria and chloroplasts is endosymbiosis: an ancestral cell took in bacteria that persisted as partners and became organelles. Mitochondria trace to bacteria related to alphaproteobacteria; chloroplasts trace to cyanobacteria.

Evidence includes organelles with their own circular DNA, bacterial-like ribosomes, division by fission, double membranes, and genetic similarity to bacterial groups. Mitochondria are found in nearly all eukaryotic lineages (some have highly modified remnants); plastids occur in plants and many algae. Primary endosymbiosis brought a bacterium into a eukaryotic host; later endosymbiosis transferred plastids among eukaryote lineages. Eukaryotic diversity includes animals, plants, fungi, and many varied microbial lineages — not just — single-celled animals. —

8. Evolution and development

Chapter 18.2

Genes guide a developing body in steps

Most cells in an animal carry the same DNA, but different genes switch on in different cells and at different times. Early developmental genes regulate later genes in a hierarchy. In fruit flies, molecules placed in the egg by the mother help mark the head-to-tail axis: Bicoid is concentrated toward the front; Nanos toward the rear. These maternal signals are concentration gradients: cells — read — the local amount and switch different target genes on or off.

Then the pattern gets refined: gap genes identify broad regions; pair-rule genes mark alternating bands; segment-polarity genes define front and back within each segment. Finally, Hox (homeotic) genes specify what each region develops into. Hox genes are deeply conserved across animals, and their order on chromosomes often corresponds to the head-to-tail regions they pattern.

Genes work in networks: a regulator can turn on some targets and repress others. A small change in the timing, location, or amount of gene expression can alter a structure without changing the gene's protein recipe. This helps explain how animals can share old developmental genes yet build different body forms. Chapter 18.3's — master regulators — are genes that can activate broad developmental programs; their effects depend on the other regulators and signals in each cell.

9. The long history of life

Chapters 22.4, 23.1, 25

Life's history is branching and uneven

Life began with microbes; oxygen-producing photosynthesis later changed Earth's atmosphere and opened new possibilities for energy use. Eukaryotic cells arose much later, followed by diverse multicellular lineages. Major groups appeared at different times, and extinctions repeatedly reshaped which branches survived. The history is not a ladder of — better — organisms: present-day microbes, plants, fungi, and animals have all been evolving for the same amount of time.

Humans belong within primates, within apes, within mammals. The human lineage arose in Africa and includes many extinct hominins. Traits such as upright walking, large brains, tool use, and culture changed at different times and in different combinations. Modern humans later spread around the world and interbred with some archaic human groups. Human genetic variation is overlapping and continuous; it does not divide humanity into neat, separate biological boxes.

Plants, fungi, animals, and many microbial eukaryotes are branches of a much older eukaryotic tree. — Protist — is a convenient informal label for many diverse eukaryotes, not one single exclusive branch. A tree helps us see both unity through common ancestry and diversity through millions of years of separate evolution.
